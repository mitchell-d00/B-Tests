THE CONTRIBUTION IS NOT THE 
BYLINE 
 
Role-Enacted Human–AI Collaboration, Intellectual Provenance, and 
Accountable Research 
 
Benjamin Gelhaus | Independent researcher | Object-Oriented Cognition and Continuity 
(OOCC) 
 
Public discussion working paper · Revised edition v1.1 · 7 November 2026 
 
Developed in dialogue with Avishai Roif’s Ethical Use of AI in Academic Research, with a 
conceptual extension drawn from Functional Layers (29 October 2026). 
 
Abstract 
 
Research integrity depends on knowing both who takes responsibility for a final claim and how 
that claim developed. This paper proposes that the roles assigned to artificial intelligence—and 
enacted through the researcher's questions, responses, corrections, and willingness to 
reconsider—are functional variables that can influence the course of human–AI collaboration. A 
designation such as “tool,” “critic,” “instructor,” or “collaborator” establishes expectations; the 
way participants subsequently interact establishes how those expectations operate. The 
resulting feedback can affect which objections are raised, which proposals are pursued, and 
which revisions become part of the work. The thesis joins two complementary inquiries: 
intellectual provenance, which reconstructs who or what introduced, developed, checked, and 
adopted an idea; and role enactment, which investigates how the interaction produced those 
contributions. The proposal builds on the author's Functional Layers framework and emerged 
during an unexpected exchange about Avishai Roif's proposed ethics code. OOCC's 
historical-learning and source-recovery records supply illustrative cases involving copied AI 
explanations, conversation branches, preserved corrections, retrieval comparisons, and 
cross-index integrity. A prospective factorial experiment is proposed to separate the effects of 
role labels from enacted collaborative behavior and to assess scientific accuracy, constructive 
disagreement, correction uptake, and cost. The empirical value of these interventions remains a 
research question; their roles and outcomes can be made observable and tested. 
 
Keywords: human–AI collaboration; role enactment; Functional Layers; intellectual provenance; 
scholarly authorship; feedback; research integrity; verification; source-governed continuity. 
 
1. Research Question and Central Argument 
 
The central problem is simple: academic authorship establishes who accepts responsibility for a 
publication, while intellectual provenance reconstructs how the underlying ideas were 
developed. Both matter for a credible research record. A paper can accurately identify its 
accountable author and still leave a reader uncertain about where a decisive objection, analogy, 
hypothesis, or revision entered the work. 
 
This paper adds a second question. What determines which ideas are proposed and which 
criticisms become influential during collaboration? The author's working hypothesis is that 
interaction roles help condition those outcomes. Role designation establishes a set of 
expectations; role enactment reinforces or contradicts them through actual behavior; the 
feedback between participants shapes the next steps. These mechanisms can be studied by 
observing what changes when role labels, interaction rules, and responses to disagreement are 
varied while other conditions are held constant. 
 
The paper therefore advances two linked propositions. First, intellectual contribution can be 
reconstructed as a source-linked history of origination, development, verification, and human 
acceptance. Second, the organization of the human–AI relationship may alter that history by 
changing the opportunities for criticism, inquiry, correction, and conceptual development. An 
accurate account of contribution examines both the events and the conditions in which they 
occurred. 
 
2. A Scholarly Exchange That Sharpened the 
Question 
 
Avishai Roif's discussion draft Ethical Use of AI in Academic Research (6 November 2026) 
centers accountability in the human researcher. Article 2 assigns responsibility for the claims in 
a paper to its author; Article 8 requires each consequential step of an argument to be justified 
and checked; Article 4 concerns AI as a research instrument; and Article 11 calls for disclosure 
of AI use. This structure supplies a useful foundation for understanding the ethical obligations 
that accompany AI-assisted work (Roif, 2026). 
 
In a subsequent exchange with the author, Roif offered a clarification that materially improved 
the present argument: Article 2 asks the researcher to make the claim their own through 
understanding, adoption, and verification; it does not demand that the researcher invented every 
initial idea. He also affirmed that AI interactions can be treated as research data when those 
interactions are themselves being studied. That clarification resolves a significant portion of the 
initial interpretive disagreement. 
 
Roif then posed a more difficult question: if AI's use is described through the methods and 
disclosure, what does the designation “contributor” add? His analogy was to a satellite, 
calculator, or search engine. The present paper accepts the distinction between institutional 
authorship and the description of an instrument's use, then asks whether a role description can 
itself affect the behavior of the research process. The term “collaborator” may function as an 
interaction instruction as well as a descriptive label. 
 
The distinction is practical. Calling an AI a collaborator can cue expectations of initiative, 
criticism, alternative hypotheses, and reciprocal revision. Calling it a calculator can cue 
expectations of narrow execution and a final answer. Actual treatment is equally consequential: 
a researcher who declares openness to criticism but dismisses every inconvenient finding 
enacts a directive relationship; a researcher who invites criticism, evaluates it, and updates a 
claim enacts a collaborative relationship. The designation and the enacted behavior are 
separately observable. 
 
This reframes the conversation as a shared research opportunity. Roif's question directs 
attention to the standards by which a human researcher adopts a claim. Functional Layers 
directs attention to the interaction conditions that influence how the claim develops. Together 
they invite an empirical investigation of whether, when, and how research outcomes change. 
 
3. Role Assignment and Role Enactment 
 
A functional role is an assignment of expected operations within a larger process. A research 
assistant retrieves relevant information; a critic challenges assumptions; an instructor builds 
comprehension; a collaborator shares a sustained inquiry through proposals, objections, 
revisions, and evidence checks. The same model may be asked to perform several roles, but 
performance depends on its capabilities, source access, governing constraints, and the 
exchange that follows. 
 
Three variables make the role observable. Assigned role records the explicit designation and 
instructions provided to the AI. Enacted role records how the human and AI actually behave 
across turns: whether questions permit disagreement, whether mistakes are acknowledged, 
whether alternatives are examined, and whether criticism can affect the next action. Observed 
contribution records what happened: a relevant objection, changed argument, corrected 
calculation, rejected hypothesis, verified source, or altered decision. 
 
The enacted role belongs to both participants. A human researcher can request candid criticism 
and then refuse every correction. Conversely, an AI can be described as a tool while the 
researcher consistently invites open criticism and thoughtful revision. The label, the instructions, 
and the human's behavior may therefore have separable effects. Their interaction is the 
experimental target. 
 
An everyday principle captures the point: actions speak louder than words. A stated 
collaborative role gains functional meaning when the participants repeatedly enact it. That 
interaction can make disagreement a useful contribution to the work rather than a failure to 
satisfy an expected answer. A correction becomes valuable when the researcher examines it, 
checks the supporting evidence, and decides what to change. 
 
The proposed term is role-conditioned functional behavior: differences in observable operations 
and research outcomes associated with explicit role designation, enacted interaction 
expectations, and feedback practices under matched conditions. The claim is causal in ambition 
and conditional in scope. The experiment should identify which mechanism matters, for which 
tasks, and at what cost. 
 
4. Functional Layers: A Framework for Investigating 
the Relationship 
 
The author's Functional Layers research, documented in an R4 evidence-first edition dated 29 
October 2026, investigates whether the causal organization of interacting functions can produce 
capabilities that are poorly described by listing components in isolation. Its unit of explanation is 
the relationship among functions: direction of influence, timing, feedback, persistent state, 
constraints, and the effect on later decisions (Gelhaus, 2026, Functional Layers). 
 
The framework provides an operational way to examine the present question. A researcher asks 
a question; an AI generates a candidate; the researcher responds; a correction may alter 
subsequent retrieval; the next response may then differ. A collaborative role is expressed 
through this sequence of interactions. The important variables are what information moves 
between the participants, what each participant does with it, and what changes when feedback 
is removed, delayed, or reorganized. 
 
Functional Layers also treats the human as part of the interacting research system whenever 
the human is actively guiding inquiry. That matters in the current case. The researcher's 
willingness to entertain a disconfirming result may be as important as the model's ability to 
produce one. Measuring AI output alone would leave part of the mechanism unexamined. 
 
The Functional Layers manuscript already proposes comparisons between linked and isolated 
processes, persistent and missing corrections, human-guided and automated retrieval, and 
connected and disconnected workflows. Role enactment supplies an additional relational 
intervention: keep the task and model constant while changing the way the human and AI 
organize criticism, acceptance, and follow-up. An improvement in accuracy, useful corrections, 
or conceptual development would then motivate deeper causal testing. 
 
The framework also identifies relevant tradeoffs. Directness, speed, creativity, factual fidelity, 
user effort, and conversational openness may respond differently to the same intervention. A 
collaborative treatment might improve error detection while increasing time spent deliberating; it 
might stimulate more alternatives while making final decisions harder. The research should 
report these dimensions separately. 
 
5. The Accidental Development of This Thesis 
 
The paper's own history illustrates why role and provenance belong together. The author began 
with a disagreement about whether institutional recognition determines the occurrence of an 
intellectual contribution. He asked an AI assistant to help develop a public reply to Roif's paper. 
The assistant suggested historical examples involving Semmelweis, Wegener, Marshall, and 
Warren and challenged an overgeneralized claim about institutional motives. The author 
accepted the correction while retaining his original argument. 
 
That exchange expanded into a discussion of practical research capacity, the author's learning 
history, and the OOCC archive. The human requested that selected evidence be assembled for 
scholarly inspection. A seven-document review packet was prepared and shared; the discussion 
unexpectedly produced a separate thesis about accountable authorship and intellectual 
provenance. The author then recognized that the new paper had emerged through precisely the 
kind of human–AI interaction it described. 
 
Roif's thoughtful reply generated a further revision. His clarification of Article 2 narrowed the 
doctrinal disagreement. His challenge about the word “contributor” prompted the human to 
connect the exchange to Functional Layers and to articulate a new insight: a designation 
establishes a role, while the human's actions and the continuing feedback enact that role. The 
present revised edition records this change as an identifiable step in the thesis's development. 
 
Within the visible exchange, the human supplied the central argument, selected research 
directions, decided which qualifications to accept, authorized publication, and initiated the new 
hypothesis. The AI contributed candidate examples, criticism, comparison ideas, editorial 
structure, and draft language. Roif's external response provided a substantive corrective and a 
motivating question. This qualitative record demonstrates a traceable development sequence; 
an independently frozen and consented conversation archive would support finer-grained 
scientific analysis of timing and causal attribution. 
 
6. Source-Grounded Cases from OOCC 
 
OOCC's archival and crawler work provides practical examples for studying provenance and 
interaction. The reported cases below retain their original scope. They are research leads and 
bounded findings drawn from dated source work. The public OOCC review packet offers curated 
case summaries and an evidence register; restricted records can be assessed through an 
appropriately authorized source-review process. 
 
6.1. Original wording and later reuse 
 
A Human Learning Crawler (HLC) case describes an original human recovery analogy, an 
assistant's technical explanation, and later reuse of AI wording in a human-role message. The 
sequence makes attribution a question of event history rather than the apparent speaker of a 
single later excerpt. The case invites a source-level check of which ideas originated where, how 
they were developed, and how the human subsequently used the explanation. 
 
6.2. Multiple branches and correction history 
 
Crawler A10 reported ten selected conversation-graph checks comprising 1,592 mapped nodes, 
including 1,411 nodes along selected-current ancestry and 181 outside that path. Such offpath 
records can contain alternative attempts, abandoned hypotheses, and corrections. A 
depth-crawler case separately preserved the distinction between an assistant's 
acknowledgment of a requested lossless reorganization and independent evidence of a 
completed result. These findings motivate procedures that track both the path followed and the 
conditions needed to verify it. 
 
6.3. Negative retrieval results 
 
A V3 retrospective comparison reported directly relevant retrieval results of 4/35 for literal 
search, 6/35 for expanded search, and 3/35 for graph-hop retrieval. In that scoped sample, 
expanded retrieval performed best. Keeping this finding visible helps prevent the attraction of a 
sophisticated relational method from becoming a substitute for comparative evaluation. 
 
6.4. Agreement between indexes 
 
An OOCC intersection report recorded matching sets of 673 unique association identifiers 
across two parsed views. It also distinguished identical mirrored records from independent 
confirmation. The result provides a case for testing record consistency and a reminder to 
validate the meanings of relationships separately from their identifier integrity. 
 
These cases show why a detailed contribution record can be useful. They also offer concrete 
tasks for a controlled study: detecting copied authorship, recovering omitted alternatives, 
evaluating a correction, selecting an effective retrieval method, and distinguishing consistent 
records from independent evidence. 
 
7. A Provenance Record with Role Context 
 
The original paper proposed an Origin–Development–Verification–Responsibility (ODVR) 
record. The revised theory retains those four elements and adds role context as a relational field 
recorded across them. The resulting representation preserves both what happened and how the 
participants' operating expectations influenced its development. 
 
Origin records the earliest observed appearance of a candidate idea within a defined source 
collection, including the original actor or message role and any preceding question. 
Development records substantive modifications, objections, alternatives, and feedback. 
Verification records the source reads, calculations, tests, and independent checks actually 
performed. Responsibility records the human researcher who evaluated, adopted, revised, or 
rejected the claim and accepted responsibility for its publication. 
 
Role context adds the initial designation, active instructions, actual human treatment, 
critical-feedback opportunities, corrections, and subsequent changes in direction. For example, 
a source-linked record might show that an AI was explicitly invited to challenge a proposed 
explanation; the AI raised a specific objection; the human checked a cited source, accepted part 
of the criticism, and revised the hypothesis. The record could also show that the human 
dismissed a criticism or that the assistant's proposed objection failed verification. 
 
Each recorded event should preserve source identity, revision, participant role, sequence, 
relation to predecessor events, evidence status, and the researcher's disposition. Where the 
available corpus starts after the idea first appeared or source access is restricted, the record 
specifies that boundary. This is important because a local earliest appearance establishes a 
source position within the collected material; a literature-wide originality claim requires separate 
research. 
 
8. A Direct Experiment: Does the Assigned and 
Enacted Role Matter? 
 
The initial demonstration is deliberately simple. Present two fresh instances of the same AI 
model with the same research task and materials. In the collaborative condition, encourage 
candid disagreement, independent alternatives, criticism of weak assumptions, and reciprocal 
examination of objections. In the directive condition, frame the model as a narrowly instructed 
instrument carrying out the researcher's chosen plan. Compare the quality of the work, including 
the human's subsequent decisions. This practical demonstration is the invitation emerging from 
the discussion with Roif. 
 
A stronger experiment distinguishes the effects of words from the effects of treatment. Use a 
two-by-two design with two labels—“tool” and “collaborator”—crossed with two interaction 
practices—directive and critical-dialogic. In the directive condition, the human asks for task 
execution and provides tightly scoped follow-up. In the dialogic condition, the human requests 
objections, checks proposed alternatives, and responds substantively when the AI disagrees. All 
conditions preserve factual accuracy requirements, honest uncertainty, and researcher 
accountability. 
 
Recruit participants or use carefully standardized human interaction scripts, and counterbalance 
assignment across comparable tasks. Match models, context, source access, task difficulty, 
time budgets, and tool permissions. Record the messages and explicit role instructions with 
consent. Include deliberate false premises, incomplete arguments, conflicting sources, and 
open-ended hypothesis development. Independent reviewers should assess outcome quality 
using preregistered criteria while blind to the experimental condition where feasible. 
 
Primary outcomes include justified disagreement rate, useful error detection, factual accuracy, 
evidence fidelity, uptake of valid corrections, quality of the final human judgment, and the 
number of unsupported confident claims. Secondary outcomes include diversity of hypotheses, 
perceived collaborative usefulness, participant learning on held-out tasks, latency, and total 
verification labor. Report human and model effort together. 
 
The factorial design supports four useful interpretations. A label effect would suggest that 
designation itself matters. An enactment effect would suggest that interaction behavior matters 
even when labels differ. An interaction effect would suggest that words and treatment reinforce 
one another. Weak or mixed effects would clarify the situations in which the approach offers 
value. These outcomes can refine the Functional Layers account and help distinguish a real 
causal mechanism from a memorable anecdote. 
 
The resulting research program contains two related comparisons. The first examines how 
interaction roles affect the development of claims. The second examines whether ODVR plus 
role-context records enable third-party reviewers to reconstruct those claims more accurately 
than competent summaries, full-text search, and strong modern source-linked memory. One 
experiment investigates production; the other investigates preservation and auditability. 
 
9. Implications for Research Integrity 
 
Roif's position provides a useful separation of duties: the human researcher adopts and 
answers for the final research claim. The present proposal examines how that claim was 
developed, which relationships contributed to its development, and how much detail a methods 
or disclosure statement should preserve. These inquiries can coexist within a 
human-accountable publication practice. 
 
A general disclosure identifying ChatGPT as an assistant communicates the presence of a tool. 
A role-aware research history can further communicate that the author invited adversarial 
review, that a specific objection changed the argument, and that the human verified the resulting 
correction. Such a record has potential value to readers assessing the strength and 
development of the argument, particularly in work where human–AI interaction is itself the 
subject of inquiry. 
 
This extension strengthens Article 8's guiding principle. Each consequential inference should 
have evidence; each consequential assertion about how the inference developed should also be 
traceable. For research in which collaboration is central, this means preserving the difference 
between origin, criticism, acceptance, source verification, and final human responsibility. 
 
The key practical proposition is therefore direct: describing an AI's role influences expectations, 
enacting that role through conduct influences feedback, and measuring the resulting changes 
can reveal what forms of interaction improve research. Scholarly rules can continue to specify 
who signs and defends the manuscript while methodology records the functional history that 
preceded the signature. 
 
10. Conclusion 
 
A name can assign a role, but the relationship gives that role its practical meaning. A researcher 
who welcomes criticism, verifies objections, and is prepared to revise a claim establishes 
different interaction conditions from one who asks only for execution of a predetermined task. 
The causal effects of those conditions can be examined with matched experiments. 
 
The original question of this thesis was who contributed what to an idea. The revised question is 
also how the working relationship influenced what was contributed. Functional Layers offers a 
framework for analyzing the feedback among participants; OOCC offers a provenance approach 
for reconstructing the resulting decisions. Together they provide a testable research agenda 
connecting role assignment, enacted behavior, intellectual development, verification, and 
accountable publication. 
 
The exchange with Roif is itself part of the recorded development. His clarification corrected the 
initial interpretation of Article 2, and his question about the term “contributor” helped generate 
the role-enactment hypothesis. In this research process, constructive disagreement became a 
source of further inquiry. The next step is to test whether comparable interaction conditions 
produce similarly useful effects under controlled circumstances.
